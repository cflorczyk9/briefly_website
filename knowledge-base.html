Knowledge base

What an AI knowledge base for financial advisors should do.

By Connor Florczyk · Updated September 30, 2026

Client documents, email, and meeting notes already sit inside your firm. Briefly reads them the way an analyst would and keeps one picture of every client in the book, from a web app the firm signs into. Ask which clients hold a concentrated position over $500K and get an answer with a name, a number, and the statement it came from, the same way you'd get an answer about one household.

Which clients hold a concentrated position over $500K?
Patel, $684,300 in employer stock (31%), Q2 statement
Okafor, $558,900 in a single legacy position (24%), Q2 statement
Lindqvist, $511,000 in concentrated equity (22%), Q1 statement

Wealth firms already have plenty of data. Client facts live across CRM records, emails, meeting notes, custodial statements, financial plans, and spreadsheets, plus whatever an advisor happens to remember, and none of those systems share a model of the relationship.

A knowledge base gives the firm that model. It connects people, households, entities, accounts, goals, concerns, events, tasks, and source citations, so every workflow starts from the same understanding of the client. Ask a question about one household or about every household in the book, and the answer comes from the same place.

Ask across your whole book, not one client file.

Most tools answer a question about whatever client file happens to be open. Briefly checks every household in your book for the same question, then answers it the way it would answer about one client, with a name, a number, and the document behind it.

The questionWhich clients hold a concentrated position above $500K? One question, checked against every household in the book, not just the one open on screen.
The answerPatel holds $684,300 in employer stock, 31% of the account. Okafor holds $558,900 in a single legacy position, 24%. Lindqvist holds $511,000 in concentrated equity, 22%.
Where it comes fromEach figure links back to the document it came from. Patel's Q2 statement, filed July 8. Okafor's Q2 statement, filed July 2. Lindqvist's Q1 statement, filed April 14.

The same pattern holds for any cross-client question, whether it's about concentrated positions, upcoming distributions, or clients who mentioned selling a business. Briefly reads the statements, transcripts, and notes already filed for each household and answers from what's actually there, with the file it read linked under every claim.

This works because every document lands in a household's own record first, then gets checked across the whole book whenever a question calls for it. Filing an email into the Patel file doesn't just help the next Patel question. It also becomes one of the records checked the next time someone asks about concentrated positions, upcoming distributions, or anything else that spans the book. A CRM field or a folder of PDFs can't do this on its own, because nothing connects the file you're looking at to the dozens of others you'd need to check by hand.

Why this matters in wealth management.

  • Households are becoming more complex as wealth transfers across generations.
  • Advisors are responsible for more relationships without proportional support staff.
  • What advisors know about a client often stays in individual memory instead of firm infrastructure.
  • Generic AI summaries are risky unless they are traceable to source material.

Briefly is built to answer inside that reality without asking a firm to change how it works. We set up the web app for your firm and connect your Salesforce and Outlook, so the documents, email, and notes are already there. Your client data stays in a private environment for your firm on AWS. The AI models that read it run on zero-retention terms and never train on it. That holds whether the question comes up in the brief before a meeting or mid-call about a household nobody on the call remembers well. The advisor opens one app and doesn't wait for someone else to pull the file.

Why CRMs are not enough.

CRMs are important systems of record, but they are not built to reconstruct a relationship across every source. The details that matter most often live in a note, a forwarded email, a PDF, a meeting transcript, a planning document, or the memory of a senior advisor.

Search helps when you already know what to look for. A knowledge base helps when you need the system to surface what matters before you ask.

A CRM also only knows what someone typed into it. It doesn't read the email you forwarded, the PDF you saved to a folder, or the transcript from last week's call, not unless someone copies the details in by hand. Briefly reads those files directly, so the client picture grows every time a document gets filed, not every time someone remembers to update a record.

Neither one learns from being corrected. If an advisor tells a CRM once that a client goes by a nickname, or that the firm calls it a planning conversation and never a sales meeting, someone has to remember to make the same fix again the next time it comes up. Briefly keeps a correction like that as a standing rule instead. One firm did exactly this with a single phrase, telling Briefly to say planning conversation instead of sales meeting, and every brief that firm has received since has followed it, for every advisor on the team.

Your brain does not have enough RAM for this job.

What a knowledge base should include.

The concentrated-position example above pulled from one category, portfolio positions. A full client file covers more ground than that.

  • Family and household relationships, including heirs and decision makers.
  • Professional network, including CPAs, attorneys, trustees, and centers of influence.
  • Planning themes such as liquidity, philanthropy, estate complexity, concentrated positions, and tax deadlines.
  • Open loops, follow-ups, unresolved concerns, and promises made in prior meetings.
  • Source citations so advisors can verify before using anything with a client.

None of this shows up on its own. A knowledge base only holds what gets filed into it, so the categories above are really a checklist for what to point Briefly at first, statements, planning documents, and the emails and notes an advisor already has sitting in a folder.

A meeting brief is one thing Briefly drafts.

A meeting brief is useful because it gives the advisor an edge before the conversation starts, but the brief draws on the same client picture behind the whole-book questions above it. Open tasks, portfolio changes, client sentiment, life events, and the professional network around a client all come from files that were filed for other reasons first. The brief just pulls the relevant pieces together and cites where each one came from. See an actual brief, with names and firms changed.

Each meeting adds more source-backed memory to the client picture, and meeting prep gets faster with every cycle because there's less new material to review each time. The client picture gets richer, and the firm becomes less dependent on any one person's memory.
